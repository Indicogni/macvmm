# Retrieval 19: virtio-net without a networking entitlement

## R1
Q: A guest posts a transmit chain of two readable descriptors, 12 bytes and 1600 bytes. What does our virtio-net do with it, and what goes in the used ring?
A: It drops the frame and counts it in `tx_dropped`: the total, 1612 bytes, is over the 1526-byte cap (a 12-byte header plus a 1514-byte frame), so nothing is allocated or sent. The chain still goes back on the used ring, with length 0, as every transmit chain does; the device never stops or resets over one bad frame.

## R2
Q: A frame arrives from the peer while the guest's receive queue is empty. What does the device do, and which counter changes?
A: It drops the frame and adds one to `rx_dropped_no_buffer`, with a rate-limited warning. It never waits for the guest to post a buffer: a waiting RX thread holds the device lock, and anything the guest does that needs that lock (such as resetting the device) then waits for it, which deadlocks the VM.

## R3
Q: A transmit chain's header starts with the bytes `00 04`. Which field is non-zero, and what happens to the frame on our device?
A: Byte 1 is `gso_type`, here 4, which asks for segmentation offload. We negotiated no offloads, so the driver must send 0 there; the device drops the frame and counts it in `tx_dropped`. Byte 0, `flags`, is 0 and fine.

## R4
Q: A guest posts a receive chain with one 64-byte writable descriptor, and a 60-byte frame arrives. What does the device write, and what does it report?
A: Nothing is written: the chain needs room for 12 + 60 = 72 bytes. The device puts the chain on the used ring with length 0 and counts `rx_dropped_small`. Had the buffer been 72 bytes or more, it would have written a 12-byte header (all zero except `num_buffers = 1`) then the frame, and reported 72.

## R5
Q: You start a VM with `--net unixgram:$T/b.sock,peer=$T/a.sock`, and `$T/b.sock` already exists. List the outcomes for: a regular file; a socket owned by another user; a socket another running VM is bound to; a socket left behind by a VM that crashed.
A: The regular file, the other user's socket and the live socket are all refused with exit code 2, before any VM exists, and left untouched. Only the last, a stale socket of yours (a test `connect` fails with `ECONNREFUSED`), is removed and bound again.

## R6
Q: A third program binds `$T/c.sock` and sends ARP frames to VM B's `$T/b.sock`, while B was started with `peer=$T/a.sock`. What does B's guest see, and where do you look to prove it?
A: Nothing. B's backend compares every datagram's source path with the peer's, byte for byte, drops the mismatch and counts it in `rx_dropped_foreign` (shown by `--stats`), with a rate-limited warning naming the sender's path. Connecting the socket would not have helped: on macOS a connected datagram socket still receives from other senders.

## R7
Q: The device strips 10 header bytes from each transmitted frame while the driver writes 12. What destination MAC address does the receiver read for a broadcast frame?
A: Two bytes too early: the last two header bytes (`num_buffers`, which the driver writes as 0 on transmit) followed by the first four `ff` bytes, so `00 00 ff ff ff ff`. That is not a broadcast and not the receiver's address, so its network stack discards the frame silently; every other field is shifted by two bytes as well.

## R8
Q: `macvmm` holds no networking entitlement. Does that stop a compromised `macvmm` from opening a connection to the internet? Why or why not?
A: No. On macOS an ordinary process needs no entitlement to open network connections; "no network entitlement" only says what we asked for. Stopping it takes containment: a sandbox the kernel enforces on the process (chapter 25). `vmnet.framework` is gated by root or the restricted `com.apple.vm.networking`, but plain sockets are not.
